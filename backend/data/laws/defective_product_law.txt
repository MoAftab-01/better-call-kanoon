INDIAN DEFECTIVE PRODUCT LAW — CONSUMER PROTECTION AND PRODUCT LIABILITY

In India, the legal framework for defective products is primarily governed by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (replacing the 1986 Act), along with provisions under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and tort principles of negligence and strict liability. The Consumer Protection Act introduces the concept of "product liability," holding manufacturers, service providers, and sellers responsible for harm caused by defective goods or deficient services. A product is considered defective if it has a manufacturing defect, a design defect, or fails to provide adequate warnings or instructions. The Act covers both tangible goods and services, providing a broad definition of "consumer" to include anyone who buys goods or hires services for consideration.

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019, establishes a three-tier redressal mechanism: District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions (District Forums) handle disputes up to INR 1 crore; State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions (State Forums) handle disputes between INR 1 crore and INR 10 crore; and the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) handles disputes exceeding INR 10 crore. A consumer who receives a defective product can file a complaint within two years from the date of cause of action. The complaint may seek compensation for the defect, repair or replacement of the product, refund of the price, or damages for any injury or loss caused by the defect. The burden of proof generally requires the consumer to show that the product was defective and that the defect caused the harm.

Under the Act, a manufacturer is strictly liable for a defective product, meaning the consumer does not need to prove negligence—only that the product was defective and caused harm. This is a significant shift from traditional contract-based remedies, where the consumer had to establish breach of warranty or misrepresentation. The manufacturer can defend itself by showing that the defect was caused by the consumer's misuse, that the consumer altered the product after purchase, or that the defect was unavoidable despite all reasonable care in manufacturing and design. Sellers and service providers are also liable if they sell a product with a known or discoverable defect, or if they fail to provide adequate instructions or warnings.

In addition to statutory remedies under the Consumer Protection Act, Indian courts have applied common law principles of negligence and strict liability in product liability cases. The Supreme Court in various rulings has held that manufacturers owe a duty of care to consumers. If a product is inherently dangerous—such as electrical appliances, pharmaceuticals, or vehicles—the manufacturer must exercise a high standard of care. Failure to test adequately, failure to warn of known risks, or failure to recall a dangerous product can result in substantial compensation awards, including for medical expenses, loss of income, pain and suffering, and punitive damages in extreme cases.

The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, further extend product liability to online marketplaces. E-commerce platforms must ensure that sellers provide accurate product descriptions, display relevant safety information, and honor return and refund policies. The rules require platforms to establish grievance officers and to respond to consumer complaints within a specified timeframe. A consumer who receives a defective product purchased online can file a complaint either against the seller, the platform, or both. The platform's liability depends on whether it acted as an intermediary or directly sold the product. The rules aim to create transparency and accountability in digital commerce, aligning with the broader goal of consumer protection under the 2019 Act.
