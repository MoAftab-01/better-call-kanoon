POLICE STOP AND SEARCH RIGHTS IN INDIA — CRIMINAL PROCEDURE CODE PROVISIONS

Under Indian law, the police do not have an unlimited right to stop, detain, or search any individual on the street. The primary legal framework governing police powers is the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC), specifically Sections 41 to 43 (arrest without warrant in certain cases), Sections 100 and 102 (search and seizure), and Section 149 (preventive detention). Additionally, the Constitution of India, particularly Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty) and Article 20(3) (protection against self-incrimination), imposes constraints on arbitrary police action. Any search or detention that violates these protections can be challenged in court and may lead to the exclusion of evidence.

A police officer may stop and question a person under Section 41 of the CrPC only if there is a reasonable suspicion that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a cognizable offence. The suspicion must be based on objective facts—not merely on appearance, caste, religion, or personal bias. The Delhi High Court and Supreme Court have repeatedly emphasized that arbitrary detention without probable cause violates Article 21. If a police officer stops a person without reasonable grounds, the individual has the right to ask for the officer's identification, the reason for the stop, and to request that the officer inform a family member or legal representative.

When it comes to search, Section 100 of the CrPC requires that a search of a person or premises must generally be conducted in the presence of independent witnesses—two respectable persons from the locality, unless the circumstances make this impossible. The officer must prepare a written record of the search, listing every item seized, with signatures from the witnesses and the person searched. Failure to follow this procedure can render the search illegal and the seized evidence inadmissible. For searches in public places without a warrant, the officer must obtain reasonable grounds from the circumstances of the case; a general instruction or suspicion without more is insufficient.

The right to remain silent and to legal representation is also protected. Article 22 of the Constitution guarantees that an arrested person has the right to consult a lawyer of their choice and to be informed of the grounds of arrest. The Supreme Court in Joginder Kumar v. State of UP (1994) held that arrest should not be made merely to harass or intimidate; it must be necessary and proportionate. If police exceed their authority—whether by unlawful detention, improper search, or coercive interrogation—the affected person may file a complaint before the Human Rights Commission, seek bail, or file a petition under Article 226 or 32 for habeas corpus or quashing of proceedings.

In recent years, courts have expanded the interpretation of police accountability. The Supreme Court in D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997) laid down comprehensive guidelines for arrest and detention, including mandatory medical examination, maintenance of arrest memos, and informing the arrested person's family within 12 hours. These guidelines are now treated as part of the law. A police stop that ignores these safeguards—such as failing to explain the reason, refusing to allow legal consultation, or conducting a search without witnesses—may be challenged as a violation of constitutional rights, and the police officer may face disciplinary or criminal action for misconduct.
