INDIAN PENAL CODE SECTION 420 — CHEATING AND DISHONESTLY INDUCING DELIVERY OF PROPERTY

Section 420 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (now replaced by Section 318 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023), deals with the offence of cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property. The classic formulation reads: "Whoever cheats and thereby dishonestly induces the person deceived to deliver any property to any person, or to make, alter or destroy the whole or any part of a valuable security, or anything which is signed or sealed, and which is capable of being converted into a valuable security, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine." This provision has been a cornerstone of fraud prosecution in India for over a century.

To establish an offence under Section 420 (or its modern equivalent), the prosecution must prove three essential ingredients: (1) the accused cheated someone; (2) the cheating was done with dishonest intent; and (3) as a result of the deception, the victim was induced to deliver property or alter a valuable security. "Cheating" is defined in Section 415 of the IPC (now Section 317 of BNS) as deceiving someone with the intent to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss. It requires an active misrepresentation—either by words, conduct, or concealment of facts—rather than mere silence, unless there is a legal duty to disclose.

In practice, Section 420 is invoked in a wide range of fraud cases: real-estate scams where developers sell the same flat to multiple buyers; investment frauds where returns are falsely promised; online scams involving forged documents or fake websites; and business frauds involving misrepresentation of financial health. The Supreme Court of India has clarified in multiple judgments—including in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992) and later rulings—that the mere breach of a contract or failure to fulfill a promise does not automatically amount to cheating under Section 420. There must be proof of dishonest intent at the time the representation was made. If the accused genuinely believed the representation to be true but failed to deliver, it is a civil dispute, not a criminal offence.

The punishment for cheating under Section 420 is imprisonment for up to seven years, plus a fine. The offence is cognizable and non-bailable in serious cases, meaning the police may arrest without a warrant, and bail is not a matter of right. The severity of punishment reflects the legislature's intent to protect property rights and maintain trust in commercial and personal transactions. Under the new Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), Section 318 retains the same core elements but updates the language and aligns penalties with other property offences, maintaining the seven-year maximum imprisonment and fine.

Victims of cheating under Section 420 may file a First Information Report (FIR) with the police, who will investigate and file a charge sheet. The case then proceeds through the magistrate or sessions court, depending on the severity and jurisdiction. Alongside criminal proceedings, victims may also file a civil suit for recovery of the defrauded property or compensation. The Evidence Act, 1872, allows for documentary evidence—including contracts, emails, bank transfers, and witness testimonies—to establish the chain of deception and delivery. Legal practitioners advise victims to preserve all records promptly, as delays can weaken evidence and allow the accused to dissipate assets or destroy proof.
